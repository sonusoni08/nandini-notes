Structure & Development of Anther and Pollen Grain

🔬 Anther Structure

The anther is a bilobed, tetrasporangiate structure borne on the filament. Each lobe contains two microsporangia arranged in a dithecous pattern.

  • Epidermis — outermost protective layer
  • Endothecium — fibrous thickening aids dehiscence
  • Middle layers (2–3) — ephemeral, nutritive during early development
  • Tapetum — innermost nutritive layer; secretes enzymes, callase, and sporopollenin precursors
  • Sporogenous tissue — microspore mother cells (MMCs)

🌱 Microsporogenesis

The process of formation of microspores from the microspore mother cell (2n) by meiosis.

  • MMC (2n) undergoes meiosis I & II
  • Produces a tetrad of four haploid microspores
  • Tetrads are usually tetrahedral or isobilateral
  • Tapetum provides nutrition via orbicules (Ubisch bodies)
  • Microspores separate after callase dissolves callose wall

🪐 Pollen Grain Structure

A mature pollen grain (microgametophyte) consists of:

  • Exine — outer, sculptured wall of sporopollenin (extremely resistant)
  • Intine — inner, pectic-cellulosic layer
  • Apertures (colpi/pores) — sites for germination and water exchange
  • Generative cell (n) — divides to form two male gametes
  • Vegetative (tube) cell (n) — forms the pollen tube

⚙️ Microgametogenesis

Development from microspore → mature male gametophyte:

  • Microspore nucleus divides (mitosis I) → vegetative & generative nuclei
  • Cell wall forms between them; generative cell floats in vegetative cytoplasm
  • Generative cell undergoes mitosis II in tube or in pollen → 2 sperms
  • 2-celled pollen is shed in most angiosperms; 3-celled in grasses
Key point — Tapetum: The tapetum is the most important nutritive layer of the anther wall. It supplies sporopollenin, pollen-kitt (sticky coating), and recognition proteins to the developing pollen grains. Loss of tapetal function leads to male sterility.

Structure and Types of Ovules

📐 General Structure of an Ovule

  • Funicle — stalk attaching ovule to placenta
  • Hilum — junction of funicle and ovule body
  • Raphe — fused portion of funicle in anatropous ovules
  • Integuments — one (unitegmic) or two (bitegmic) protective coats
  • Micropyle — pore at the apex through which pollen tube enters
  • Chalaza — basal region where integuments attach to nucellus
  • Nucellus — nutritive tissue; surrounds the embryo sac
  • Embryo sac (female gametophyte) — located inside the nucellus

Types of Embryo Sacs & Ultrastructure of Mature Embryo Sac

🌀 Megasporogenesis

  • Megaspore mother cell (MMC, 2n) in the nucellus
  • Undergoes meiosis → linear tetrad of 4 haploid megaspores
  • Usually chalazal megaspore is functional; 3 others degenerate
  • Functional megaspore develops into the female gametophyte (embryo sac)

🏗️ Ultrastructure — Mature Embryo Sac (Polygonum type)

The 7-celled, 8-nucleate mature embryo sac consists of:

  • Egg apparatus (micropylar end):
    • 1 Egg cell (n) — the female gamete
    • 2 Synergids (n) — with filiform apparatus; guide pollen tube; secrete chemotropic substances
  • Central cell: 2 polar nuclei (later fuse → secondary nucleus 2n)
  • Antipodals (chalazal end): 3 cells (n) — nutritive function

🧬 Megagametogenesis (Polygonum Type)

  • Functional megaspore → 3 mitotic divisions
  • 1 → 2 → 4 → 8 free nuclei
  • 4 nuclei migrate to micropylar end, 4 to chalazal end
  • One nucleus from each pole → polar nuclei in center
  • Cell walls form → 7-celled embryo sac
  • Synergids develop filiform apparatus (invaginated cell wall) for tube guidance

📌 Embryo Sac Diagram

Egg cell — female gamete; participates in syngamy with sperm 1
Synergids (2) — filiform apparatus at micropylar end; secrete chemotropics
Polar nuclei (2) — fuse with sperm 2 → triploid (3n) endosperm nucleus
Antipodals (3) — chalazal end; nutritive role; may degenerate early

Types of Placentation

Definition: Placentation is the arrangement of ovules inside the ovary on the placenta (region of ovary wall/axis where ovules are attached).

🔹 Marginal

Ovules borne along the ventral suture (margin) of a monocarpellary ovary. Placenta forms a ridge.

Example: Pisum sativum (pea), Leguminosae

🔹 Axile

Multilocular ovary; ovules attached to the central axis (fused walls of carpels). Most common type.

Example: Hibiscus, Citrus, Tomato

🔹 Parietal

Ovules borne on the inner walls (peripheral) of a unilocular syncarpous ovary. Placentae on wall.

Example: Cucurbita, Argemone, Brassica

🔹 Free Central

Unilocular ovary; ovules borne on a central column arising from the base with no septa.

Example: Dianthus, Primula

🔹 Basal

Single ovule borne at the base of a unilocular ovary.

Example: Compositae (sunflower), Polygonaceae

🔹 Superficial

Ovules borne all over the inner surface of locular walls in multilocular ovaries.

Example: Nymphaea (water lily)

Pollination: Mechanisms and Adaptations

Pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from the anther to the stigma of the same or different flower of the same species.

💨 Anemophily (Wind Pollination)

Adaptations:

  • Flowers small, inconspicuous, no scent/nectar
  • Pollen grains: dry, light, smooth, non-sticky, abundant
  • Stigma large, feathery, exserted to catch pollen
  • Anthers versatile (oscillate freely)
  • Flowers often unisexual, arranged in catkins

Examples: Grasses, Pinus, Betula, wheat, maize

🐝 Entomophily (Insect Pollination)

Adaptations:

  • Flowers large, brightly coloured, scented
  • Nectar and nectaries present
  • Pollen: sticky, spiny, with pollen-kitt
  • Stigma sticky, positioned to contact insect body
  • Elaborate floral shapes for specific insects

Examples: Rosa, Brassica, Salvia, orchids

🦅 Ornithophily (Bird Pollination)

Adaptations:

  • Flowers large, tubular, red/orange (birds see red)
  • Copious, dilute nectar
  • No scent (birds lack strong olfaction)
  • Robust floral structure to bear bird weight
  • Protandry/protogyny to encourage cross-pollination

Examples: Strelitzia, Bombax, Erythrina

🦇 Chiropterophily (Bat Pollination)

Adaptations:

  • Flowers dull-coloured (white/cream), open at night
  • Strong musty/fruity scent
  • Large, sturdy flowers with exposed stamens
  • Nectar-rich

Examples: Kigelia, Baobab, Agave

💧 Hydrophily (Water Pollination)

Adaptations:

  • Pollen: long, ribbon-like, water-proof
  • Flowers small, inconspicuous
  • No periath or much reduced
  • Epihydrophily: transfer at water surface (Vallisneria)
  • Hypohydrophily: transfer below water (Ceratophyllum)

🐌 Other Agents

  • Malacophily — snails/slugs (Arum)
  • Myrmecophily — ants
  • Cantharophily — beetles (primitive flowers)
  • Cleistogamy — self-pollination in closed flowers (Viola, Commelina)

Pollen–Pistil Interaction

1. Pollen Landing on Stigma

  • Stigma surface: wet (with stigmatic exudate) or dry
  • Pollen hydrates and swells on compatible stigma
  • Stigmatic proteins recognize compatible/incompatible pollen
  • Recognition involves S-gene products on pollen wall and stigma surface

2. Pollen Tube Germination

  • Compatible pollen germinates → pollen tube emerges through aperture
  • Tube grows through stigma → style → ovary
  • Growth: apical (tip) growth driven by Ca²⁺ gradient
  • Nutrients from style (transmitting tissue)
  • Generative cell divides → 2 sperms in tube

3. Tube Guidance to Ovule

  • Chemotropic guidance by γ-amino butyric acid (GABA) and other signals
  • Synergids secrete LURE peptides to attract the tube to micropyle
  • Tube enters ovule through micropyle (porogamy)
  • Rarely through chalaza (chalazogamy) or integument (mesogamy)

4. Double Fertilization

  • Pollen tube bursts in synergid → releases 2 sperms
  • Sperm 1 + Egg (n) → Zygote (2n) → embryo (syngamy)
  • Sperm 2 + 2 polar nuclei (2n) → Primary endosperm nucleus (3n) → endosperm (triple fusion)
  • Double fertilization is unique to angiosperms
Note: Double fertilization was first discovered by S.G. Nawaschin (1898) in Lilium and Fritillaria.

Self-Incompatibility (SI)

📖 Definition

A genetically controlled mechanism that prevents self-fertilization by inhibiting pollen germination or tube growth when genetically identical pollen lands on the stigma.

Controlled by a single, highly polymorphic S-locus with multiple alleles.

🏷️ Types of Self-Incompatibility

TypeControlInhibition siteExample
Sporophytic SI (SSI)Sporophyte (diploid) genotype of pollen parentStigma surfaceBrassica, Ipomoea
Gametophytic SI (GSI)Gametophyte (haploid) genotype of pollenStyleNicotiana, Petunia, Prunus

🧪 Molecular Mechanism (GSI — RNase-based)

  • Pistil expresses S-RNases encoded by S-locus
  • Matching S-RNase enters compatible pollen tube → not neutralized → RNA degradation → tube dies
  • Non-matching S-RNase → neutralized by SLF (S-locus F-box) proteins → tube survives

🌾 Overcoming SI

  • Bud pollination — stigma not yet mature, SI not established
  • Irradiation of pollen (weakens S-gene expression)
  • Mentor pollen technique
  • Stub pollination — stylar grafting
  • Important in plant breeding to produce hybrids from SI parents

Significance of Self and Cross Pollination

✅ Self-Pollination (Autogamy)

Significance:

  • Maintains genetic purity of the variety
  • Useful in regions with no pollinators
  • Economical — no need for elaborate floral structures
  • Ensures seed set even in isolation
  • Useful for producing true-breeding lines in plant breeding

Disadvantages:

  • Leads to inbreeding depression (reduced vigour)
  • Reduces genetic variability → poor adaptability
  • Accumulates deleterious recessive alleles

🔀 Cross-Pollination (Allogamy/Xenogamy)

Significance:

  • Introduces genetic variability → raw material for evolution
  • Produces hybrid vigour (heterosis)
  • Eliminates deleterious alleles over generations
  • Increases adaptability to environmental change
  • Basis of commercial hybrid seed production

Disadvantages:

  • Dependent on external agents — unreliable
  • Wastage of large amounts of pollen
  • Useful genes may be lost in recombination

🌿 Devices Promoting Cross-Pollination

  • Dicliny (unisexuality) — monoecious (Cucurbita), dioecious (Carica)
  • Dichogamy — maturation of stamens & pistils at different times
    • Protandry: stamens mature first (Helianthus)
    • Protogyny: pistil matures first (Aristolochia)
  • Heterostyly — different style/stamen lengths (Primula)
  • Herkogamy — physical barrier between anther & stigma
  • Self-incompatibility — genetic barrier
